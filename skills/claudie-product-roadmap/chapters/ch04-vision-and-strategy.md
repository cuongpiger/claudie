# Chapter 4: Establishing the Why with Product Vision and Strategy

## Core Idea
Your roadmap needs a foundation: a vision of the future for your customer *and* your organization, plus a strategy — expressed as objectives and key results — for achieving it. Split that strategy into OKRs and you direct product effort toward measurable outcomes rather than specific outputs.

## Frameworks Introduced

- **Mission / Vision / Values distinction**: Vision is your ultimate destination; mission tells you which direction to follow to reach it; values are your compass, telling you right from wrong but not which way to travel.
  - When to use: Before writing any roadmap, to stop mission statements masquerading as vision statements.
  - How: Mission = the intent you hold *right now* and the purpose driving you to realize your vision; it must reflect what you do for someone else — typically your customers, not your shareholders. Vision = a longer-term *outcome* that impacts the lives of the people your product serves and your organization; a statement painting a future reality. Values = beliefs and ideals that guide behavior and shape culture (how people behave when no one is watching).
  - Why it works / failure mode: Most roadmaps detail *what* you intend to deliver with no vision/mission/values context, forcing stakeholders to piece it together themselves. Vision statements commonly fail by being self-centered — "be the best ___."

- **The four elements of a well-crafted mission statement**: Value, Inspiration, Plausibility, Specificity.
  - When to use: As a checklist when writing or auditing a mission statement.
  - How: **Value** — what value does your mission bring to the world? **Inspiration** — how does it inspire your team to make the vision a reality? **Plausibility** — is it realistic and achievable? If not it is disheartening and people won't work at it; if it seems achievable people will work their tails off. **Specificity** — is it specific to your business, industry, and/or sector, so it resonates with the organization?

- **Value Proposition Template** (Geoffrey Moore's template from *Crossing the Chasm*, also known as the Elevator Pitch template, adapted for roadmapping):
  - When to use: To create a product vision from scratch, or as a gut-check on an existing one before it drives strategy and roadmap.
  - How — fill in each blank:
    ```
    For:          [target customer]
    Who:          [target customer's needs]
    The:          [product name]
    Is a:         [product category]
    That:         [product benefit/reason to buy]
    Unlike:       [competitors]
    Our product:  [differentiation]
    ```
    Then tie it to company strategy by adding: `Supports our: [objective(s)]`
    Then compress to a vision statement:
    ```
    A world where the [target customer] no longer suffers from the [identified problem]
    because of [product] they [benefit].
    ```
    Some teams pare it down further to just benefit plus differentiator: `To [benefit realized] by [product differentiator]`.
  - Why it works / failure mode: It forces the *who* (target customer), the *what* (problem it solves), and the *why* (benefit received). Downside: plug-and-play vision statements when the vision should be unique and specific to your product and business — use the template as scaffolding, not the final voice.

- **Objectives and Key Results (OKRs)**: Objectives are specific *qualitative* goals; key results are *quantitative* measures of progress toward achieving those objectives.
  - When to use: To connect vision to roadmap — identifying business objectives tied to your vision is what makes the vision implementable rather than a direction-less aspiration.
  - How — three guidelines for roadmapping:
    1. Everything on the roadmap must be tied to at least one of your objectives.
    2. Stick to a manageable number of objectives — fewer than five is most effective (three to five for metrics purposes).
    3. Focus on outcomes, not output.
    There can be multiple key results per objective, since it may take different measures to determine whether an objective is met.
  - Origin: First implemented by Andrew Grove at Intel in the early 1980s (*High Output Management*); used today at Google, Uber, Zynga, LinkedIn. See also Christina Wodtke's *Radical Focus*.

- **Vision statement minimum components**: The target customer (*who?*) and the benefit or need(s) addressed (*why?*); optionally what makes it unique (*how is it different?*).

## Key Concepts
- **Mission**: The intent you hold right now and the purpose driving you to realize your vision — not what you value, not a vision of the future.
- **Vision**: A longer-term outcome that impacts the lives of the people your product serves and your organization; why the organization exists.
- **Values**: Beliefs and ideals that guide behavior and shape culture — a compass, not a direction.
- **Product vision**: Clarifies why you are bringing a product to market and what its success will mean to the world and the organization; the *raison d'être* of the effort and the basis of the roadmap.
- **Product strategy**: The bridge connecting high-level vision to the specifics of the roadmap, usually taking the form of objectives.
- **Output**: The stuff you produce, physical or virtual, for a specific type of customer — e.g. car seats for babies.
- **Outcome**: The difference your stuff makes — e.g. keeping your child safe in the car. "Outcomes are the difference made by the outputs" (Deb Mills-Scofield).
- **Key results / KPIs**: The quantitative measures of progress toward an objective; the metrics that define how a product's success will be measured.
- **Feature factory**: A team that releases feature after feature with no tie back to the reason for those features.
- **Duality of company and customer benefit**: Internal and external objectives should be mutually reinforcing — improved usability gives the customer a better experience while giving the business decreased churn and increased LTV.

## Mental Models
- **Think of vision as the destination, mission as the direction, values as the compass.** A compass tells you which way is north — it never tells you where to go.
- **Use "what kind of product do we need to attain those business objectives?"** (Bryan Dunn, Localytics) when translating a business objective into product work.
- **Use "ask why enough times"** on any proposed theme, feature, solution, program, or initiative — it always comes back to one of the 10 universal business objectives.
- **Think of a roadmap as a story, not a release plan.** "A roadmap is not a release plan. A roadmap is a sequence of stakeholder priorities and requires concept feasibility for delivery. A release plan requires rigorous scope definition and engineering capacity planning" (Michael Salerno, Brainshark). The roadmap has to tell the product story; it doesn't have to get the days right.

## Anti-patterns
- **Vision trapped in one person's head**: If the CEO has a vision she never communicates, decisions made in her absence will struggle. A vision does nobody good until it is shared.
- **Mission statements posing as vision statements**: Very common; conflating the two loses the destination.
- **Self-centered vision ("be the best ___")**: Fails to name the customer or the benefit.
- **Too company-centric an internal vision**: Microsoft's "a personal computer on every desk running Microsoft software" worked in the 1980s only because customer benefit was implied; today it reads as Microsoft-centric and no longer serves.
- **Output-focused roadmaps / feature factories**: Releasing feature after feature with no tie back to a reason — you shipped a lot, but did it achieve the outcome you sought?
- **Tracking too many metrics**: If reviewing your data at a high level takes longer than one hour each week, you are tracking too much. Conversely, tracking revenue alone tells you something is broken only when it is too late.
- **Sharing an internal-objectives roadmap with customers or channel partners**: They do not care about internal metrics or technical platform work — only how you add value to them.
- **Treating a roadmap as a release plan**: Date-precise commitments belong in release and project plans.

## Worked Example

**Value Proposition Template → vision statement (Wombat Garden Hose)**

```
For:          homeowner landscape enthusiasts
Who:          need a reliable hose that survives repeated use
The:          Wombat Garden Hose
Is a:         water delivery system
That:         offers reliable operation under any conditions
Unlike:       the competition
Our product:  is indestructible and offers uninterrupted water delivery
```
Compressed to a vision statement: *A world where American landscape enthusiasts [target customer] can have a more predictable and automatic [identified problem] watering system that can perfect their lawns [benefit] with an effective water delivery system [product].*
Pared to benefit + differentiator: *To perfect American lawns by perfecting water delivery.*

**SpaceX — vision → business objective → themes → key results**

- **Vision**: "SpaceX was founded under the belief that a future where humanity is out exploring the stars is fundamentally more exciting than one where we are not... with the ultimate goal of enabling human life on Mars." Benefit made explicit: "The alternative to an extinction event is to become a multi-planet species." Product vision: *create a self-sustaining city on Mars* — Mars being "the best option among nearby planets for a self-sustaining civilization."
- **Business objective (with built-in key result)**: Emigration currently costs ~$10 billion per person, so "there is no overlap between people who want to go and people who can afford to." Objective: drive the cost to the equivalent of a house in the US — about US $200,000.
- **Themes** (answering "for this strategy to be viable, what would need to be true?"): full spacecraft reusability; refueling in orbit; propellant production on Mars; right propellant. None names a specific technology — each is a subgoal of the affordability objective.
- **Key results** for the "full spacecraft reusability" theme: overall market cost is $200,000 or lower; reusability of rocket transportation is 90% or greater; tons of propellant can be produced on Mars.

## Key Takeaways
1. Fix your vocabulary first — vision is the destination, mission the direction, values the compass — then make all three explicit and shared, not stuck in one head.
2. Audit every mission statement against Value, Inspiration, Plausibility, Specificity — and check that it describes what you do for customers, not shareholders.
3. Build product vision with the Value Proposition Template, then compress it to a one-sentence vision covering who, what problem, and what benefit.
4. Tie every roadmap item to at least one objective, and keep objectives to fewer than five so they retain focus.
5. Trace any proposed theme or feature back to one of the 10 universal business objectives by asking why enough times; if it doesn't land on one, question the work.
6. Write objectives qualitatively and key results quantitatively; expect multiple key results per objective.
7. Measure outcomes (the difference your output makes), not output — and keep the weekly metrics review under an hour.
8. Keep dates off the roadmap. Use Now / Next / Later and save specific commitments for release and project plans.
