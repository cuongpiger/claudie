# Chapter 3: Gathering Inputs

## Core Idea
Before you draw a roadmap, gather three input streams — where your product sits in its life cycle, what the market and customers actually need, and what your stakeholders contribute. Skipping this work means designing for fictional scenarios with biased assumptions.

## Frameworks Introduced

- **The Five Product Life-Cycle Phases**: New Product, Growth, Product Expansion, Harvesting, End-of-Life.
  - When to use: At the very start of any roadmapping effort, to decide what kind of input the roadmap needs and how much uncertainty you must absorb.
  - How: Place your product in one phase. Then match your input-gathering to it — New Product demands assumption-listing plus rapid prototype-and-test cycles; Growth demands prioritization for scale (including removing features that no longer serve); Expansion demands discovery of an adjacent need still linked to the core product; Harvesting demands a constant, revolving feedback loop from committed customers; End-of-Life demands wind-down planning and stakeholder communication.
  - Why it works: Each phase has a different risk/uncertainty profile. Growth is low-risk because the customer base is known and product discovery is smooth; New Product is high-risk because everything is an untested assumption. Failure mode: applying a Growth-style "prioritize the backlog" process to a New Product, where there is nothing validated to prioritize.

- **Business Model Analysis (Business Model Canvas / Lean Canvas)**: Assemble, at bare minimum, a basic business model analysis for your product before roadmapping.
  - When to use: Always, as a gate. If you cannot complete one of these canvases at a basic level, you are not ready to build your roadmap.
  - How: Work through the pillars — problem and solution, value proposition, unfair advantage, key metrics, customer segments, distribution channels, costs, revenue model, key partners, key resources. Use **Lean Canvas** (Ash Maurya) for startups or new products; use **Business Model Canvas** (Alex Osterwalder) for existing products or growing businesses.
  - Why it works / failure mode: Without it you start with a faulty map — "like a 15th-century explorer trying to reach new lands with a map that shows only half the world." Every team member, not just the product manager, needs this baseline understanding.

- **Customer Development Model** (Steve Blank): Sequence your exploration — Customer Discovery → Customer Validation → Customer Creation → Company Building.
  - When to use: When you need to validate a problem before committing roadmap capacity to a solution.
  - How: Discovery — get out of the building and confirm the core problem directly with the target audience (what they call the problem is often just a symptom of a larger one). Validation — prove at least some of the target audience will pay. Creation — demonstrate early success grows into sustained demand and a sales pipeline. Company Building — expand the initial team into formal departments once product and sales justify it.

- **Voice of the Customer merged top-10 lists** (Jackie Bavaro, Asana): Each customer-facing team creates its own top-10 list; all teams merge their lists into one overall prioritized list.
  - When to use: When multiple internal teams each claim to represent the customer.
  - How: Collect per-team top-10s → merge and rank collaboratively (the merge empowers teams to be directly involved in ranking) → uplevel from specific feature suggestions into broader themes → add tactical and strategic factors to set the roadmap.

## Key Concepts
- **Product core**: The team that designs, builds, ships, and/or maintains a particular product or version — at minimum a Product Manager/Owner, designers, and engineers.
- **User role**: A description focused on jobs and functions — the primary action taken by a particular user (e.g. student, instructor on Lynda.com).
- **User type**: How a user interacts with the product, or that user's permissions — end user, general admin, master admin, manager, operator, viewer.
- **Persona**: A representation of a user embodying the characteristics, feelings, and preferences of a user set; deals with softer characteristics, usually depicted with an image plus descriptive attributes.
- **Buyer vs user**: Users use the product; buyers buy it. In B2B/enterprise they are usually different people; in B2C they are usually the same person.
- **Stakeholder rings**: Concentric layers around the product core, organized by knowledge of the product space, influence over decisions, or frequency of interaction with the product team.
- **Cash cow product**: A Harvesting-phase product with proven product-market fit, long market tenure, and a solid, growing customer base (e.g. Google AdWords).
- **Sunsetting**: The End-of-Life act of winding a product down and taking it off the market — a legitimate use of a roadmap, requiring the same strategy and planning as growth.

## Mental Models
- **Think of the business landscape as a map you are about to navigate.** Do the up-front work or you sail with half the world missing — you will end up somewhere, just not where you meant to go.
- **Use "problem, then outcome" as your experience test.** The novice focuses on features; the seasoned pro focuses on problems; the best pros ask "if we solve that problem, what's the outcome we want to see?"
- **Use roles to categorize, personas to empathize.** Roles tell you which customers the product must serve; personas take you a level deeper into what each of them actually needs.
- **Treat every stakeholder as a two-way trade.** Each one both benefits from seeing the roadmap and contributes input to it — enter every stakeholder conversation knowing both sides of that exchange.

## Reference Tables

### The five product life-cycle phases and the roadmap input each demands

| Phase | What the roadmap does here | Risk / uncertainty | Input to gather |
|---|---|---|---|
| **New Product** | Outline and drive creation of the first version | High — uncharted territory | Assumptions, then rapid prototyping and testing; validation (or lack of it) decides which themes/features make the roadmap |
| **Growth** | Prioritize team activities so all are operating to address scale; get to as many customers as possible | Low in both risk and uncertainty | Known customer base and well-understood problems; product discovery is smoother. May mean reorganizing features/themes for UX, or removing features no longer necessary |
| **Product Expansion** | Extend the core product or product line into a new area, still linked to the core but addressing an entirely new need | Medium | Evidence the existing format transfers to an adjacent market (Kayak: flights → hotels and cars) |
| **Harvesting** | Keep the cash cow useful and relevant so customers keep coming back | Low, but rising customer demands | A constant and revolving roadmapping process driven by customer feedback on continuous improvements |
| **End-of-Life** | Wind the product down with ease, dignity, and strong communication | Political / relational | Stakeholder alignment and sunset planning across the organization |

### Stakeholders: how they benefit and what they contribute

| Stakeholder | Benefit | Contribute |
|---|---|---|
| Customers | Get excited about how they will benefit in the future | Feedback on value and priorities |
| Executives | Understand how resources are used and potential ROI | Strategic context for product direction and priorities |
| Sales | Be ready to answer customers/prospects on future direction | What will make prospects buy and customers renew |
| Marketing & PR | Prepare for launch and promotion | What will get the attention of the target market |
| Customer Support | Be ready for inquiries on bugs, usability, future features | Top reasons for support calls |
| Research | Plan research to support future roadmap themes | Market and user information to inform themes and priorities |
| Other Product Teams | Sync their technical approach and release schedule with yours | Sync your approach and schedule with theirs |
| Operations, Production, Purchasing, HR | Understand future infrastructure, tooling, plant, personnel, scaling needs | Insight into those costs for proposed roadmap items |
| Finance | Understand planned spending and expected ROI | Available budget and ROI targets |
| Vendors & Technology Partners | Plan timing/volume of components and supplies, performance specs | Capacity and performance specs; insight into new materials and technologies |
| Channel Partners | Plan assortment, pricing, promotion, training, distribution | What will likely sell, timing and pricing; co-marketing plans |

## Anti-patterns
- **Delegating business understanding to the product manager alone**: Designers and engineers assume the business context is the PM's job. True in theory, wrong in practice — every team member needs a basic understanding of the business environment or decisions made without the PM present go sideways.
- **Roadmapping without customer empathy**: Trying to roadmap without deep empathy for the customer invariably sends you down the wrong path, wastes time, and — left unchecked — leads to product failure.
- **Conflating roles with personas**: Treating them as the same term loses either the categorization (which customer groups exist) or the depth (what each actually needs).
- **Assuming the stated problem is the real problem**: What a potential customer thinks is the problem is often just a symptom of a much larger one. Confirm through direct interaction before designing.
- **Coasting in the Harvesting phase**: Teams cannot sit back and rake in profits — the more customers commit to a product, the more demanding they become.
- **Hubris about existing context**: Believing you already have all the context you need wastes time, effort, resources, and money.

## Worked Example

**Lynda.com — from problem to roles, types, buyers, and personas**

1. **Problem**: A gap in the education market around professional skill development. Traditional in-person adult education is time-consuming and expensive; people want to enhance a skill set remotely.
2. **Roles** (jobs and functions): *student* and *instructor*. The platform must add value to both, or it does not solve the problem.
3. **User type** for the student: *end user* — she uses Lynda to take classes and learn skills.
4. **User vs buyer**: The student is both — she benefits from the classes and pays for them out of her own pocket (B2C pattern). Contrast: a VP of Sales buys the Salesforce subscription, but sales managers and reps are the daily users (B2B pattern).
5. **Personas** add the layer roles cannot: every student watches videos, downloads documents, and asks forum questions — but *Professional Paul* needs a payment history to submit an expense report to the employer covering his class, while *Social Sam* wants detailed profiles of classmates so he can make friends through the network. Those persona-level needs become distinct roadmap items.

## Key Takeaways
1. Identify your product's life-cycle phase first — it determines what input your roadmap needs and how much of it must be validated versus assumed.
2. Gate your roadmap on a completed business model canvas (Lean Canvas for new, Business Model Canvas for established). Cannot fill it in? You are not ready.
3. Get out of the building. Confirm the problem directly with your target audience before you design the solution — what they report is often a symptom.
4. Every item on your roadmap should address an actual customer need.
5. Separate roles (jobs/functions), user types (permissions/interaction), buyers vs users, and personas (empathy) — each answers a different roadmap question.
6. Map your stakeholders explicitly, noting both what each benefits from and what each contributes, then bring them in at the right points in the process.
7. The more information you have about the space you operate in, the more effective you are as a product leader — your job is to make sense of these inputs and build that knowledge into the roadmapping process.
