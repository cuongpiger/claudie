# Chapter 11: Relaunching Roadmaps in Your Organization

## Core Idea
Score your organization's roadmap health first, then pick between course-correcting an existing process and a full relaunch — and drive that change incrementally through stakeholder alignment, not by fiat. Every launch is eventually followed by a relaunch.

## Frameworks Introduced

- **The Six-Step Relaunch Process**: the getting-started sequence for changing how your organization roadmaps.
  - When to use: your org is either bogged down predicting the unpredictable, or has abandoned roadmaps and runs sprint-to-sprint / demand-to-demand.
  - How: see the numbered reference below.
  - Why it works: it treats process change exactly like product change — define the problem before proposing a solution, get alignment, ship small, measure, iterate.

- **Roadmap Health Assessment Checklist**: a **14-question**, **0–2 scale** self-scoring diagnostic (0 = entirely or mostly no, 2 = definitely yes). **Maximum score: 22** — because some questions are scored *negatively* (subtract).
  - When to use: Step 1, before choosing an approach.
  - How: score each question; add the "+" questions, subtract the "−" questions (overpromising and overdesigning items); compare against the bands. Each question cites the chapter to consult if the criteria are unclear.
  - Scoring bands: **18+** = great process, take Approach A to tweak and enhance. **12–17** = reasonably good shape, still Approach A, but recognize there's a lot to improve and a world-class process will take time. **11 or lower** = no process or so broken you need a new baseline — go to Approach B.
  - Why it works: it converts a vague sense of frustration into a prioritized list of specific gaps, each mapped to a chapter.

- **Approach A — Course Corrections**: use the assessment questions to identify the most promising (or most-broken) opportunities, prioritize them with a Ch7 method (critical path works well here), and **improve one part of the process at a time**. Applies both to good processes being enhanced and to bad-but-salvageable ones. It's tempting to change many things at once; with something basically functional you make quicker progress with a single focus.

- **Approach B — The Full Relaunch**: no salvageable process, so establish one. Begin at the beginning and follow the book's steps in order — **Chapter 3 (gathering inputs) through Chapter 10 (keeping it fresh)**. Even experienced teams can take **months** to land a roadmap and update process they're happy with.
  - Accelerator: the **roadmap workshop** — like a Design Sprint, it gathers key cross-functional stakeholders in a focused period, **usually a few days**, to define product vision, objectives, customer needs, and other roadmap components, producing an initial roadmap plus agreed next steps (customer validation, revision cadence).
  - An unbiased expert facilitator helps — they carry an established framework and cross-engagement experience. If you've read the book, you may be that expert in your org.

## Key Concepts
- **Roadmap health**: a scored measure of how well your current roadmapping process does the five things good roadmaps do and avoids the three things they shouldn't.
- **Approach A / Approach B**: course-correct incrementally vs. rebuild the process from a new baseline.
- **Roadmap workshop**: a multi-day, Design Sprint-style cross-functional session that produces a first roadmap and a revision cadence.
- **Alignment, not consensus**: stakeholders need to agree change is necessary, not agree on how broken things are or where to start.
- **Shuttle diplomacy**: one-on-one stakeholder discussions (often framed by a scorecard) to build a plan, rather than negotiating in a room (Ch8).
- **Product core**: the product people, engineers, and designers closest to the effort (Ch3) — the safe minimum audience for a relaunched roadmap.
- **Discovery period**: the post-release evaluation window, borrowed here to evaluate *internal* process changes.
- **Product steering committee**: a standing cross-functional group that reviews roadmap progress, actual product results, and next steps.
- **The IKEA effect / contribution obligation**: every stakeholder has a part to play in roadmapping; they usually just need to be shown how.

## Mental Models
- **Treat organizational change as a product problem**: define the problem before proposing a solution, then ship it incrementally with feedback loops — the exact discipline you apply to the roadmap itself.
- **Use "alignment, not consensus" when stakeholders disagree**: if they agree change is *necessary*, they'll give you leeway on *how*. Don't spend capital relitigating the diagnosis.
- **Think of scope-limiting as having two dials, not one**: shrink the *process* scope (fix one part at a time) or shrink the *audience* scope (start with the product core, then widen the circle, evaluating feedback after every encounter).
- **Use a cookie-cutter template as a warning sign**: any attempt to force-fit a template PowerPoint or project plan will fail; the roadmap must fit your product and your organization.

## Reference Tables

### The six-step relaunch process

1. **Assess your situation and choose an approach.** Score the 14-question Roadmap Health Assessment (0–2, max 22). 18+ or 12+ → Approach A (course corrections, one area at a time, prioritized via Ch7). ≤11 → Approach B (full relaunch, Ch3→Ch10, optionally kicked off with a multi-day roadmap workshop).
2. **Get buy-in for change from your key stakeholders.** You can't change process by fiat across departments. Use Ch8 techniques — shuttle diplomacy, co-creation workshops — to identify and fold in stakeholders. Share your assessment results; better, have each stakeholder score the checklist independently, then compare answers. Seek **alignment, not consensus**.
3. **Train your stakeholders how to contribute.** Assume they arrive expecting feature-and-date commitments. Explain what's different this time, why, and how it will be better. Every stakeholder has an obligation to contribute — most just need to be shown how. Borrow the book's principles and process steps; a prep slide deck is available at www.productroadmapping.com.
4. **Start small and work incrementally.** Look for every opportunity to demonstrate early success. Under A: pick one process area, align on what "improvement" looks like, set a goal achievable **in a few weeks** (e.g., adopt one Ch7 prioritization model, or define some short-term business objectives). Under B: limit scope other ways — a workshop takes only a few days; or limit the *audience* to the Ch3 product core, then widen slowly.
5. **Evaluate your results and align on next steps.** Run a discovery-period equivalent for the process change: gather stakeholders periodically, review what changed, and seek feedback on effects — intended or not. Decide to stay the course, change course and retry, or head back to base for a different direction. Use your cross-functional product steering committee; if there isn't one, create it. **Many such teams meet every three to six weeks** — more often isn't productive (change hasn't had time to show effects), less often prevents the team gelling and loses shared context. Tell the team you intend to keep evolving the process and expect them to surface improvements.
6. **Keep relaunching.** Change is hard for people, harder for organizations, and hardest across organizational boundaries. Expect resistance from those who don't see the need, feel threatened, or have other priorities — including your own team. Don't give up.

### What good roadmapping does / avoids (the assessment's backbone)

| Does | Avoids |
|---|---|
| Puts plans in strategic context | Promises the team isn't confident it will deliver |
| Focuses on delivering customer and organizational value | Wasteful up-front design and estimation |
| Embraces learning as part of development | Conflating roadmap with project plan or release plan |
| Rallies the organization around one set of priorities | |
| Gets customers excited about product direction | |

### Assessment questions, by dimension (with chapter references)

| Dimension | Questions | Score |
|---|---|---|
| Strategic context | Clear product vision most stakeholders can explain (Ch4); measurable business objectives most stakeholders are aware of (Ch4) | + |
| Focus on value | Roadmap focuses on customer needs (Ch5); everything on it tied to customer needs and/or business objectives (Ch2) | + |
| Embrace learning | Time allowed to learn whether solutions work before committing (Ch2); roadmap updated regularly via an evolving process (Ch10) | + |
| Rally the organization | Established process for stakeholder alignment (Ch8); objective and accepted prioritization method (Ch7); roadmap regularly presented/shared with key stakeholders (Ch9) | + |
| Get customers excited | Customer feedback sought and incorporated (Ch8); roadmap regularly presented/shared with customers (Ch9) | + |
| Avoid overpromising | Precise or "best-case" dates on the roadmap (Ch9); specific features/solutions/fixes/deliverables on the roadmap (Ch6) | **−** |
| Avoid overdesigning/overplanning | Solution thoroughly designed before a need goes on the roadmap (Ch5); project info — resources, milestones, dependencies — embedded in the roadmap (Ch9) | **−** |

## Anti-patterns
- **Changing the process by fiat**: people in other departments won't go along without question; you need alignment first.
- **Fixing everything at once**: with a basically functional process, a single focus makes quicker progress; broad simultaneous change stalls.
- **Chasing consensus on the diagnosis**: stakeholders won't agree on how broken things are or where to start — demanding that agreement burns the capital you need for the change itself.
- **Force-fitting a cookie-cutter template or project plan**: guaranteed to fail; the roadmap must fit your product and organization.
- **Steering-committee cadence extremes**: meeting more often than every three weeks means no time for effects to show; less often than six weeks loses shared context and prevents gelling.
- **Expecting the relaunch to hold**: treating it as one-and-done. You won't get it all right on the first try.

## Worked Example
**Step 1 applied — Gillian Daniel, smartShift Technologies (2014 → mid-2016).**

Situation: no controlled, predictable growth mode; solutions (bundles of technology and services), positioning, and messaging all needed redefinition. On the health checklist this scores low on strategic context (no shared vision or measurable objectives) and on prioritization — Approach B territory: start at the beginning.

She worked with the executive team to define a vision — what the company wanted to be, what problem it wanted to solve, and for whom. With that in place she proposed **business objectives** (outcomes, not outputs): revenue per customer, penetration into new markets. These became the yardstick for every proposed product or enhancement.

For prioritization she built a **scorecard** ranking each idea against each business objective on a simple high/medium/low scale, plus a quick **T-shirt sizing** from Engineering. Highest rating across multiple goals for least effort won. But the scorecard was explicitly *not* the approval gate — she used it as a framework for one-on-one executive discussions, playing out scenarios and arriving at a plan through **shuttle diplomacy**, because alignment matters more than any individual feature.

Each PM published a roadmap for their product line, weighted heavily toward the strategy — the "why" — over feature detail: "That context is necessary to make sure each team member is armed with the right information to make all of the day-to-day decisions that pop up."

Then Step 5 in action: months of execution produced solid gains in revenue per customer, validating the approach — **but margins fell as cost to serve rose**. They reevaluated goals, added **profit** (via cost reduction) to the scorecard, and the shifted scoring moved the roadmap "with little fuss." The company returned to profitable growth and significantly grew its US customer base and revenue.

## Key Takeaways
1. Score before you act: 14 questions, 0–2 each, max 22. 12+ → course-correct (Approach A); 11 or lower → full relaunch (Approach B, Ch3→Ch10).
2. Remember two questions subtract — precise dates and embedded project info actively damage your score. Absence of bad practice counts as much as presence of good.
3. Have every stakeholder score the checklist independently, then compare. Disagreement is fine; agreement that change is necessary is what you need.
4. Under Approach A, fix one part of the process at a time with a goal you can hit in a few weeks — early visible success buys support for the rest.
5. Under Approach B, compress the relaunch into a multi-day roadmap workshop, and limit the audience to the product core before widening the circle.
6. Put process evaluation on a standing cadence: a cross-functional product steering committee meeting **every three to six weeks** to review changes, actual product results, and next steps.
7. Say out loud that the process will keep evolving, and expect the team to surface improvements. Every launch is eventually followed by a relaunch.
8. Postscript guidance: share your learning to get help back; talk regularly with product peers about their dysfunctions, roadblocks, stakeholders, and decision-making; and deliberately look **outside your market or industry** — digital ↔ physical ↔ services, B2C ↔ B2B — to find practices worth adopting.
