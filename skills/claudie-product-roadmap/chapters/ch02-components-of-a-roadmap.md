# Chapter 2: Components of a Roadmap

## Core Idea
A roadmap that can rally your troops needs more than features and dates: it must tell the story of what it will be like when you achieve your vision, what it will take to get there, and how you will know if you are making progress. Build it from five **primary components**, add **secondary components** only where a specific stakeholder needs them, and keep **complementary information** off the roadmap but ready to discuss.

## Frameworks Introduced

- **Primary / Secondary / Complementary component taxonomy**: the three-tier structure of everything that could appear on (or near) a roadmap.
  - When to use: every time you build, review, or trim a roadmap.
  - How:
    1. Cover all five **primary** components first — treat them as a checklist of essentials: Product Vision, Business Objectives, Timeframes, Themes, Disclaimer.
    2. Add **secondary** components selectively, one stakeholder concern at a time: Features and Solutions, Stage of Development, Confidence, Target Customers, Product Areas. Each is optional and "every additional bit of information has the potential to backfire."
    3. Keep **complementary** information (Project Information, Platform Considerations, Financial Information, External Drivers) *off* the roadmap, but be ready to discuss it — or present it jointly with the owning stakeholder for context.
  - Why it works: the primary tier carries the *why* and the *story*; the secondary tier answers specific stakeholder anxieties; separating the complementary tier is what stops a roadmap from degenerating into a project plan. Failure mode: including every secondary component "just in case" obscures the core message with extraneous detail.

- **Themes Focus on Outcomes Rather Than Output**: derive themes by answering *"What would need to be true for our product to realize its vision and attain its business objectives?"*
  - When to use: turning vision + objectives into a roadmap backbone.
  - How: research customer problems; express each prioritized problem as a customer need or problem statement; make that the theme. Themes guide the development of solutions (features and functions) rather than naming them.
  - Why it works: a problem-shaped theme leaves the solution space open for the team while still communicating commitment and sequence.

- **Broad Timeframes Avoid Overcommitment**: use calendar quarters, half-years, or **Now / Next / Later** rather than ship dates.
  - When to use: always, for themes and solutions. Reserve specific dates for external events and hard commitments (see Ch 9).
  - How: order themes into progressively coarser buckets as they move right; end with an open "Future" column. In all cases the sequence communicates what's important now and what can wait.
  - Why it works: focusing on dates as the primary measure of success diverts attention from the iterative, uncertain process of innovation.

## Key Concepts
- **Product Vision**: How a specific sort of customer will benefit from your product when it is fully realized and ubiquitous — the guiding principle / North Star.
- **Business Objectives**: The goals and outcomes the product will accomplish; what will be measurably different for your organization.
- **Timeframes**: Broad time buckets (quarters, half-years, Now/Next/Later, "Future") that convey sequence and priority without committing to ship dates.
- **Theme**: A customer need or problem that organizes the work and deliverables of your team; the backbone of the roadmap.
- **Disclaimer**: An explicit caveat that everything in the roadmap is subject to change without notice — protects you from broken-promise accusations and protects the customer by making change visibly likely.
- **Features and Solutions**: The specific deliverables — features, capabilities, quantities, enhancements, updates, optimizations — that fulfill the needs and solve the problems identified in the themes.
- **Stage of Development**: A label (discovery, design, prototyping, pre-production, beta) signaling how "real" an item is and what kind of input or preparation it invites.
- **Confidence**: An explicit indication of how likely you are to address an item or theme in the next release; offsets the sense that anything on the page is a promise.
- **Target Customers**: Which customer type a theme serves, called out when the product serves more than one.
- **Product Areas**: Annotation of themes or features by area of a large/complex product; areas may carry their own business objectives.
- **Complementary information**: Categories not formally part of the roadmap (project, platform, financial, external drivers) that stakeholders expect you to know and be ready to discuss.

## Mental Models
- **Use the primary components as a checklist, the secondary components as a menu**: essentials must all be present; optional ones are ordered à la carte per stakeholder.
- **Think of each secondary component as a load-bearing answer to one stakeholder's anxiety**: sales/marketing want stage of development; executives want confidence; engineering wants product areas. If no stakeholder is asking, leave it off.
- **Think of stage of development as a "realness" dial**: "discovery/design/prototyping" invites input on sketches and material choices; "pre-production/beta" tells sales, marketing, support, and channel partners to start getting ready.
- **Use "there is no template"**: the components are a vocabulary, not a boilerplate. Every roadmap is different depending on what you're trying to communicate and to whom.

## Anti-patterns
- **Treating any example roadmap as a template**: "This is not a roadmap template." A standardized roadmap doesn't work across all organizations; forcing one hides the story your product actually needs to tell.
- **Including every secondary component you can**: Extraneous detail obscures the core message; every additional bit of information has the potential to backfire.
- **Putting the project plan on the roadmap**: The internal project plan's level of detail on timing and resources is "both overwhelming and distracting" to most roadmap stakeholders.
- **Specific ship dates for themes and solutions**: Makes dates the measure of success and invites the commitment trap. Reserve dates for external events and hard commitments.
- **Listing features for far-out themes**: Later themes are too early in development to be that certain; naming features there manufactures false precision — keep the *problem* as the focus.
- **Shipping a roadmap without a disclaimer**: Removes both your protection against broken-promise accusations and the customer's signal that change is likely. (Even Intel, whose public roadmap has little detail, prefaces it with a prominent disclaimer — check with finance/legal on your own policy.)
- **Adding confidence percentages that add nothing**: If broad timeframes plus stage of development already communicate tentativeness, percentages "would add little more insight."

## Worked Example
**The Wombat Garden Hose Co. roadmap, built up component by component**

Brief: you work for a garden hose company and have been asked to create a new product for affluent Americans.

| Step | Component added | Content |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Company vision | Focused on Americans and on making landscapes beautiful — the grounding for everything after |
| 2 | Research | 63% of Americans surveyed indicate insufficient irrigation — identifies what matters most to achieving the company vision |
| 3 | **Product Vision** | Derived almost directly from that insight: a garden hose that delivers perfectly-watered landscapes |
| 4 | Customer problems | Work with potential customers surfaces the most important problems in achieving perfectly-watered landscapes — first problem: *hoses kink, split, and leak* |
| 5 | **Themes** | Each prioritized problem becomes a theme. Problem #1 (kink/split/leak) → theme **Indestructibility**. Later themes include **Infinite Extensibility** and **Fertilizer Delivery** |
| 6 | **Timeframes** | Themes ordered into broad buckets: half-year periods first, then a single column for all of 2018, ending with **"Future"** |
| 7 | **Disclaimer** | Everything subject to change without notice |
| 8 | Details/exhibits | Only the *first* theme carries feature detail; carefully chosen diagrams, mockups, demos make plans tangible |

Secondary components, applied selectively:

| Secondary component | Decision on the Wombat roadmap | Reasoning |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Features and Solutions | Listed **only** for the Indestructibility theme | That product is nearly ready to ship; later themes are too early to be certain, so the theme's problem stays the focus |
| 2. Stage of Development | Indestructible hose shown as **pre-production** | Signals sales, marketing, support, and channel partners to get ready to market, sell, distribute, and support it |
| 3. Confidence | **Omitted** | Stage of development through 2018 (and its absence in 2019) plus broad timeframes already communicate tentativeness; percentages would add little |
| 4. Target Customers | Called out only for **Infinite Extensibility** and **Fertilizer Delivery** | Initial offering is clearly American consumers; those future themes target the **professional landscaper** market — a new target market worth flagging |
| 5. Product Areas | **Omitted** | Each theme is developed holistically by one team with no division between components, so omitting it keeps the communication uncluttered |

Complementary information, applied sparingly:
- **Project Information**: since the Indestructible hose is in pre-production, execution questions are natural. The full production schedule stays off the roadmap, but **a quick mention of the number one risk** sparks the right conversation with internal stakeholders who can help mitigate it. All other complementary information waits for targeted conversations with the right stakeholders.

Real-world roadmaps shown for comparison: Gov.uk, Chef Automate, GitHub, Bazel, an insurance company, Office 365.

## Key Takeaways
1. Cover all five primary components — Product Vision, Business Objectives, Timeframes, Themes, Disclaimer — before adding anything else.
2. Derive themes by asking "What would need to be true for our product to realize its vision and attain its business objectives?" and express them as customer needs or problems.
3. Map each prioritized customer problem to exactly one theme, in priority order — that mapping is the roadmap's backbone.
4. Use broad timeframes that widen as you go right (half-years → a year → "Future"); sequence, not dates, communicates priority.
5. Add feature-level detail only where the product is close to shipping; for later themes, keep the problem as the focus.
6. Choose secondary components per stakeholder, and justify each omission as deliberately as each inclusion — omitting Confidence and Product Areas can be the right call.
7. Keep complementary information (project, platform, financial, external drivers) off the roadmap, but know it cold and be ready to co-present it with the owning stakeholder for context.
8. Always carry a disclaimer; check your organization's policy with finance or legal.
