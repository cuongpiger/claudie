## Core Frameworks & Mental Models

### The central claim
A roadmap is **a strategic communication tool — a statement of intent and direction — not a project plan**. It is a *prototype of your strategy*. Commit to **outcomes, not output**. Per Marty Cagan: "It's all about solving problems, not implementing features."

The test: if your roadmap could be handed to engineering as a build order, it's a release plan wearing a roadmap's clothes.

### Component taxonomy (Ch 2 — the book's backbone)
Build every roadmap from these three tiers:

| Tier | Components | Rule |
|---|---|---|
| **Primary** (always) | Product Vision, Business Objectives, Timeframes, **Themes**, Disclaimer | Omit any one and it stops working as a roadmap |
| **Secondary** (situational) | Features/Solutions, Stage of Development, Confidence, Target Customers, Product Areas | Add only what your audience needs to decide |
| **Complementary** (by audience) | Project Information, Platform Considerations, Financial Information, External Drivers | Per-audience layer, not part of the foundation |

**Strive for balance**: every added detail buys clarity and costs commitment. More detail ≠ better roadmap.

### Themes over features (Ch 5)
Write roadmap rows as themes, in this format:

> **Ensure [result] for [stakeholder]**

A theme is a **customer need**, decomposable into subthemes. Features are *probable solutions*, not promises. Themes survive learning; features don't.

**A feature earns a place on the roadmap only when it is**: (1) a probable solution you genuinely intend, (2) infrastructure work that must be visible, or (3) carryover from a prior period. Otherwise it belongs beneath the roadmap, not on it.

**Use `Ensure [result] for [stakeholder]` when** you catch yourself writing a feature name in a roadmap cell. If you can't restate it as a result for someone, you don't yet know why it's on the roadmap.

### Vision and strategy (Ch 4)
- **Mission** = your intent. **Vision** = the outcome you seek. **Values** = beliefs. Destination / direction / compass.
- A good mission has four elements: **Value, Inspiration, Plausibility, Specificity**.
- **Value Proposition Template** (Geoffrey Moore): `For [target customer] who [need], the [product] is a [category] that [key benefit]. Unlike [alternative], our product [differentiator].`
- **OKRs**: objectives are qualitative and inspiring; key results are numeric and falsifiable.
- **The 10 universal business objectives** group into *Sustainable Value / Growth / Profit* — use them as a checklist so a theme always ties to an objective. (Full table in ch04.)

### Prioritization (Ch 7 — densest chapter)
| Framework | Use when |
|---|---|
| **Critical Path** | Sequencing is the real constraint — what must precede what |
| **Kano** | Deciding how much of each *kind* of need to fund (expected / normal / exciting) |
| **Desirability, Feasibility, Viability** | Fast triage across three lenses; 1–3 each, composite out of 9 |
| **ROI Scorecard** | You need a defensible ranking: `Value ÷ Effort = Priority`, Value = customer needs + org goals, discounted by Confidence % |
| **MoSCoW** | *Communicating* a decision already made — it does not make one |

**Tools Versus Decisions**: a framework informs the decision; it never makes it. **Dependencies, Resources, and Promises** affect *scheduling*, not *priority* — don't let them corrupt your scores.

**The six bad (but common) ways to prioritize**: your or someone else's gut · analyst opinions · popularity · sales requests · support requests · competitive me-too features. All six share one failure: letting someone outside the product define your strategy.

### Alignment and buy-in (Ch 8)
**Alignment ≠ Consensus.** You want alignment (everyone pulling the same direction) and collaboration — never consensus (everyone agreeing), which produces mush and delay.

**Shuttle Diplomacy**: meet stakeholders *one at a time, before the group meeting*, surface each one's goals and objections privately, then arrive with a plan that already absorbs them. Works because people defend positions in groups but reveal interests alone. Structure it with the **Shuttle Diplomacy Canvas** and **GROW** (Goals / Reality / Options / Way forward).

**Authorship is the lever**: "not your plan — *our* plan." See also the **IKEA Effect** (Ch 9) — people value what they helped build.

### Presenting (Ch 9)
Use a **Modular Roadmap**: one shared foundation plus a per-audience layer. Never maintain multiple roadmaps — maintain one with audience-specific views.

**Detail-by-Distance**: the further out in time, the less detail. Confidence typically runs **Now 70–95%, Next ~50%, Later ~25%**.

**Sharing risks**: Overpromising and Underdelivering · the **Osborne Effect** (announcing the future kills the present product) · leaking differentiation to competitors.

### Keeping it fresh (Ch 10) and rolling it out (Ch 11)
- **Timeframe by product stage**: Discovery → months (Now/Next/Later); Growth → quarters; Maturity/decline → years.
- **Refresh rate must match time scale** — a quarterly roadmap gets a quarterly review.
- **Communicate change in this order: why → what → when.** Leading with *what* reads as a broken promise; leading with *why* reads as learning.
- **Roadmap Health Assessment**: 14 questions, 0–2 scale, max 22. ≥12 → Approach A (course corrections). ≤11 → Approach B (full relaunch, follow Ch 3→Ch 10 in order).

## Scope & Limits

This skill covers the book's content only (2017 edition). It is about *roadmapping* — direction-setting and communication — not delivery execution, sprint planning, or engineering estimation. For applying these frameworks to a specific product, combine with the actual customer research and business context; the book is explicit that every organization mixes and matches based on its own readiness.
