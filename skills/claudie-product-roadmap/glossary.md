# Glossary — Product Roadmaps Relaunched

**Alignment** — Shared understanding of goals, issues, and each person's role; reachable even when people still disagree. (Ch 8, Ch 11)
**Brooks's Law** — "Adding manpower to a late software project makes it later"; three-to-seven-person teams deliver most effectively. (Ch 10)
**Business Objectives** — Primary component: the measurable outcomes the product will produce for the organization. (Ch 2, Ch 4)
**Buyer vs. user** — Buyers pay for the product, users use it; usually different people in B2B, the same person in B2C. (Ch 3)
**Carryover** — Work unshipped from a previous roadmap or release plan, rolled into the next version as a subtheme. (Ch 5, Ch 6)
**Co-creation Workshop** — A structured 90-minute cross-functional session that finalizes prioritization together rather than presenting a finished plan. (Ch 8)
**Collaboration** — Individuals cooperating toward a common goal; they need not concur at every step, only on the outcome. (Ch 8)
**Complementary information** — Project, platform, financial, and external-driver context kept off the roadmap but known cold and ready to discuss. (Ch 2, Ch 9)
**Confidence** — Stated probability an item ships in its timeframe; never 100%, set per column, per item, or by separation line. (Ch 2, Ch 6)
**Confidence separation line** — A line in the Now column: above it the team is committed for the next release, below it is aspirational. (Ch 6)
**Consensus** — Group agreement used as a decision mechanism; produces decisions nobody owns. Seek alignment instead. (Ch 8, Ch 11)
**Critical Path** — Prioritization framework: from journey maps keep only the must-fix pain points; what remains is your MVP blueprint. (Ch 7)
**Desirability, Feasibility, Viability** — Prioritization framework scoring each idea 1–3 on customer value, ease of building, and organizational value; sum out of 9. (Ch 7)
**Disclaimer** — Primary component stating everything is subject to change without notice; protects you and warns the customer change is likely. (Ch 2)
**Feature factory** — A team releasing feature after feature with no tie back to the reason those features exist. (Ch 4)
**Features and Solutions** — Secondary component; allowed on a roadmap only as subthemes, and only when probable, infrastructure, or carryover. (Ch 2, Ch 6)
**IKEA Effect** — People value what they helped assemble; early involvement converts "your plan" into "our plan." (Ch 8, Ch 9)
**Iron Triangle** — The four tradeable levers — schedule, scope, resources, quality. Fail to choose which gives and quality silently gives. (Ch 10)
**Job Story** — "When [situation] / I need [desire] / So I can [result]"; used when the triggering moment matters more than the persona. (Ch 5)
**Kano Model** — Prioritization framework classifying needs by the customer's own perception of value: expected, normal, exciting. (Ch 7)
**Kano — Expected needs** — Dissatisfiers: without them your chosen customer will not buy; roughly equivalent to the critical path. (Ch 7)
**Kano — Normal needs** — Satisfiers: not strictly required, but articulated by customers once expected needs are met. (Ch 7)
**Kano — Exciting needs** — Delighters beyond expectation; they define category leaders, but decay into normal needs as expectations rise. (Ch 7)
**MoSCoW** — Must/Should/Could/Won't have; communicates launch criteria for an already-prioritized list — not itself a prioritization method. (Ch 7)
**MVP** — "That version of a new product which allows a team to collect the maximum amount of validated learning with the least effort" (Ries). (Ch 7, Ch 10)
**OKRs** — Qualitative objectives with quantitative key results; keep to fewer than five objectives and tie every roadmap item to one. (Ch 4)
**Opportunity Cost** — The important thing you never get to do because you chose something else; permanent, so sequence value first. (Ch 7)
**Opportunity-Solution Tree** — Torres's hierarchy of desired outcome → opportunities (this book's themes) → solutions → experiments. (Ch 5)
**Osborne Effect** — Pre-announcing future products suppresses current sales as buyers wait; named for Osborne Computer's 1983 collapse. (Ch 9)
**Outcome** — The measurable difference your output makes for customer and organization; the thing you commit to. (Ch 1, Ch 4, Ch 5)
**Output** — The stuff you ship — features, units, deliverables; the means, never the end. (Ch 1, Ch 4, Ch 5)
**Persona** — A representation of a user set's characteristics, feelings, and preferences; roles categorize customers, personas build empathy for them. (Ch 3)
**Pivot** — "A change in strategy without a change in vision" (Ries); revisit the roadmap immediately, not on cycle. (Ch 10)
**Primary components** — The five essentials of any roadmap: product vision, business objectives, timeframes, themes, disclaimer. Use as a checklist. (Ch 2)
**Product Areas** — Secondary component tagging items by part of the product (UI, platform, APIs); areas may carry their own objectives. (Ch 2, Ch 6)
**Product life cycle (five phases)** — New Product, Growth, Product Expansion, Harvesting, End-of-Life; each demands different inputs and carries different uncertainty. (Ch 3)
**Product roadmap** — A strategic statement of how you intend to achieve your product vision, focused on value delivered rather than features and dates. (Ch 1)
**Product vision** — How a specific sort of customer benefits when the product is fully realized and ubiquitous; the roadmap's North Star. (Ch 2, Ch 4)
**Punctuated equilibrium** — Long periods of stability broken by rapid change; the target pattern for roadmap revision. (Ch 10)
**Release plan** — Tactical execution artifact detailing which features ship when; explicitly not a roadmap. (Ch 1, Ch 5)
**ROI Scorecard** — Prioritization spreadsheet: Priority = ((customer needs + organizational goals) / effort) × confidence%. Best for long multi-factor lists. (Ch 7)
**Roadmap Health Assessment** — 14-question, 0–2 self-score (max 22; two questions subtract) selecting course correction (12+) or full relaunch (≤11). (Ch 11)
**Role (user role)** — A user described by job and function (student, instructor); used to categorize which groups the product must serve. (Ch 3)
**Secondary components** — Optional per-stakeholder detail: features and solutions, stage of development, confidence, target customers, product areas. (Ch 2, Ch 6)
**Shiny Object Syndrome** — Pursuing many parallel "number-one priorities"; what leadership calls agility the development team experiences as distraction. (Ch 1, Ch 7)
**Shuttle Diplomacy** — Meeting each stakeholder one-on-one with a draft roadmap and scorecard, before ever convening the group. (Ch 8, Ch 11)
**Stage of Development** — Secondary component labelling how "real" an item is (discovery → beta); also detects whether an item is theme or feature. (Ch 2, Ch 6)
**Subtheme** — A more specific need — or a named probable solution — nested under a parent theme; granularity is the only difference. (Ch 5, Ch 6)
**T-shirt sizing** — Relative effort estimation (XS=1 … XL=5); only the ordering matters, never the absolute duration. (Ch 7, Ch 8)
**Target Customers** — Secondary component tagging which customer type or persona each theme serves. (Ch 2, Ch 6)
**Technical debt** — Accumulated quality shortcuts that slow development and delay future roadmap items; surface it as its own theme or row. (Ch 9, Ch 10)
**Theme** — A high-level customer or system need or problem — the roadmap's backbone. Format: "Ensure [result] for [stakeholder]." (Ch 1, Ch 2, Ch 5)
**Timeframes** — Primary component: broad buckets (quarters, half-years, Now/Next/Later, "Future") that convey sequence without committing to dates. (Ch 2, Ch 4)
**User Story** — "As a [user type] / I want [desire] / So I can [result]"; supports a theme with a role-centric need. (Ch 5)
**Value Proposition Template** — Moore's For / Who / The / Is a / That / Unlike / Our product fill-in, compressed into a one-sentence vision statement. (Ch 4)
