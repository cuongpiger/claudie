# Coaching, the Pre-Investing Checklist, Mistakes & Fears

Sources: *Mutual Funds For Dummies* Ch. 3 (foundation & goals), Ch. 21 (ten
mistakes), Ch. 22 (ten fears). KB depth:
`[[Ch03 - Funding Your Financial Plans]]`,
`[[Ch21 - Ten Common Fund-Investing Mistakes and How to Avoid Them]]`,
`[[Ch22 - Ten Fund-Investing Fears to Conquer]]`.

Use this for beginners, for "how do I start", and whenever someone is about to
invest before their foundation is ready. **The biggest mistake is buying funds
before you're financially and emotionally ready** — so walk the checklist first,
then hand off to `portfolio-construction.md`.

## The pre-investing checklist (do this BEFORE buying any fund)

Work these ten prep steps; the first three are non-negotiable gates.

| # | Step | Core point |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Pay off consumer debt** | You can't reliably out-earn **14–18%** credit-card interest with an ~8% return. Clearing the balance is a guaranteed high return. Consider tapping savings (keeping an emergency cushion). |
| 2 | **Review insurance** | One catastrophe can wipe out investments. Check **life, long-term disability, comprehensive health, and liability** coverage. |
| 3 | **Define your goals** | Funds are goal-specific tools; the clearer the goal, the better the fund choice — and the clearer how much risk you actually need. |
| 4 | Know your savings rate | savings ÷ income (e.g. save $3,000 on $60,000 = **5%**). |
| 5 | Examine spending & income | To save more, usually cut spending (more feasible than earning more for most). |
| 6 | **Max tax-deferred retirement accounts** | 401(k)/403(b): contributions are federal- & state-tax-deductible, growth is sheltered, and employers may match. This is priority #1 *unless* you still carry high-interest debt. |
| 7 | Know your tax bracket | Dictates which funds make sense outside retirement accounts (see `tax-and-maintenance.md`). |
| 8 | Assess risk tolerance | Ask honestly how you'd react to a **10–50%** one-year drop before buying volatile funds. Selling after a big drop is the classic mistake. |
| 9 | Review current holdings | Look at total asset allocation across *all* accounts as one whole. |
| 10 | Consider other options | Funds aren't the only path — real estate, your own business, or paying down the mortgage may fit better. |

## The goal → vehicle map

| Goal | What to do | Vehicle |
|---|---|---|
| **Emergency reserve** | Fund this before any other goal | **3–6 months** of living expenses in a **money market fund**; up to **a full year** if income is erratic and you have no family safety net |
| **Retirement** | Start early; each decade of delay **roughly doubles** the % of income you must save for the same goal. Use tax-advantaged accounts first | Stock-heavy funds for the long horizon (see allocation formulas) |
| **Home (down payment)** | Short/medium horizon | Money market / short-term bond funds |
| **College** | **Fund your own retirement first**; keep money out of the child's name (aid formulas ignore 100% of retirement assets but penalize a child's assets heavily) | Age-of-child allocation (see `portfolio-construction.md` Step 4) |

**Time-horizon rule** (repeat of the portfolio rule, because beginners need it
early): ≤ 2–3 yrs → money market / short-term bond; 3–7 yrs → bond funds; 7+ yrs →
stock funds.

> The cautionary tale (Justine & Max): a couple rushed into five funds in a week
> after a scary seminar. The funds were fine; the *approach* was wrong — they
> skipped their employer plan (lost the match + deduction), bought income-oriented
> bond funds when they needed decades of growth, generated needless taxable income
> in a taxable account, and didn't cut spending to fund the higher savings rate
> (so the "extra" investing money was really credit-card debt at 14%). Foundation
> first.

## The ten common mistakes (Ch. 21) — and the fix

1. **No overall plan** → build the plan first: insurance, debt payoff, savings
   goals, asset allocation.
2. **Ignoring sales charges & expenses** → check loads + expense ratio before
   buying; fees are the most predictable driver of returns.
3. **Chasing past performance** → judge manager/company consistency and risk; hot
   funds cool.
4. **Ignoring taxes** → know your bracket and pick tax-appropriate funds for
   taxable money — but don't let taxes dominate.
5. **Being duped by "advisers"** → avoid commissioned salespeople; hire someone who
   sells only their advice (fee-only), or a dedicated money manager.
6. **Collection syndrome (too many funds)** → let the plan and allocation drive
   selection; you don't need many funds.
7. **Timing the market** → buy good funds gradually and stay invested.
8. **Following prognosticators** → use information, not predictions; let your goals
   and risk tolerance drive.
9. **Being swayed by major news events** → don't act on the day's headlines; at
   most buy the "sale" with spare cash.
10. **Comparing funds unfairly** → compare against a relevant index over years, not
    against cherry-picked peers over weeks.

## The ten fears (Ch. 22) — and the reframe

1. **Investing with little money** → funds/ETFs suit small investors; start with a
   hybrid/fund-of-funds (~$1,000 min, lower with auto-invest; ETFs have no
   minimum).
2. **Uninsured funds** → the risk is price volatility, not lack of insurance; funds
   can't go bankrupt. Use balanced funds if volatility scares you.
3. **Rising interest rates** → markets lead the economy 6–12 months; it's already
   priced in — don't act on rate forecasts.
4. **Missing high stock returns** → you won't out-pick the pros; don't buy
   individual stocks to beat fund managers, and never trade on inside info.
5. **Waiting to understand the economy** → macro forces are unforecastable and
   already priced in; build around your goals, not the noise.
6. **Buying the best performers** → today's winners become tomorrow's laggards;
   favor sound, currently-out-of-favor types.
7. **Waiting for the ideal entry** → you can't call the bottom; invest regularly
   and dollar-cost-average lump sums over 1–2 years.
8. **Obsessing over your funds** → monitoring ≠ obsessing; daily watching breeds
   panic-selling. Keep the big picture and diversify.
9. **Thinking you chose badly** → a decline is usually the whole category, not your
   fund; benchmark against peers before judging.
10. **Lacking performance (top-ten lists)** → those lists ignore risk and shift
    yearly; use proper evaluation methods (see `tax-and-maintenance.md`).

## How to coach
- Meet people where they are. If they haven't cleared steps 1–3, gently steer
  there before allocation talk — but acknowledge their enthusiasm.
- Normalize the fears above; most beginners hold several. Reframe with the reason,
  not just "don't worry."
- Move to `portfolio-construction.md` once the foundation is in place.
