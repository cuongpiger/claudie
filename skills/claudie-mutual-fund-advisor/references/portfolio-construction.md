# Portfolio Construction

Source: *Mutual Funds For Dummies* Ch. 10 (with goal/time-horizon logic from Ch. 3).
KB depth: `[[Ch10 - Perfecting a Fund Portfolio]]`.

Building a portfolio is four decisions in order: **allocation → tax placement →
strategy (index vs. active, buy-and-hold) → execution (which/how many funds, how
to deploy cash).** Work them in that order; each constrains the next.

## Step 1 — Asset allocation by time horizon

**Time horizon = years until you need the money.** It's the primary driver of how
much short-term loss you can absorb. Short-term money must stay stable; long-term
money can ride stock volatility because it has time to recover.

| Time horizon | Vehicle |
|---|---|
| < 2 years | Money market funds |
| 2–3 years | Money market + short-term bond funds |
| 3–7 years | Shorter-term bond funds |
| 7+ years | Stocks + longer-term bonds |

Rule of thumb the book leans on: stocks have averaged **~9%/yr over two centuries**
(some years +20–30%, some −20% or worse). That average only accrues to people who
stay invested for the long haul.

## Step 2 — Long-term allocation by age & risk personality

For retirement and other 7+ year goals, start from age and adjust by risk
personality. **Stocks % and bonds % must sum to 100.**

| Personality | Bonds % | Stocks % |
|---|---|---|
| Play it safe | = age | 100 − age |
| Middle of the road | age − 10 | 110 − age |
| Aggressive | age − 20 | 120 − age |

**Personalities:**
- **Play it safe** — little experience/success with stocks, risk-averse; fine if
  you've already saved enough to afford lower growth.
- **Middle of the road** — some experience and comfort with growth investing.
- **Aggressive** — proven comfort with stock risk, *or* behind on saving and
  needing the money to work harder.

> Tip: if you're inexperienced, start at play-it-safe and migrate toward a riskier
> mix as your comfort grows. Gradual beats abrupt.

## Step 3 — Split stocks home vs. abroad

Concentrating all stock money in one country (even the US) is riskier than
diversifying. Overseas share **of the stock sleeve**:

| Personality | International % of stocks |
|---|---|
| Play it safe | 20% |
| Middle of the road | 33% |
| Aggressive | 40–50% |

Convert to whole-portfolio %: `international_of_portfolio = stocks% × intl%`.

## Step 4 — College money (child's age)

Same growth-then-safety arc, but the horizon tracks the **child's** age, not the
parent's. Bond portion:

| Personality | Bonds % |
|---|---|
| Play it safe | 60 + child's age |
| Middle of the road | 45 + child's age |
| Aggressive | 30 + child's age |

Rest to stocks (≥ 1/3 overseas), de-risking as the child nears college.
⚠️ Money in a **child's name** and the wrong vehicles can hurt financial-aid
eligibility and raise taxes — fund your own retirement first.

## Step 5 — Strategy: index vs. active, and buy-and-hold

**Buy-and-hold beats market timing.** Timing requires calling tops and bottoms,
which only works in hindsight; timers reliably underperform and pay more in taxes
and effort. Pick solid funds and stay invested through downturns.

**Index funds beat ~75% of comparable active funds over 10+ years**, chiefly on
cost:
- Avg US stock fund expense ratio ≈ **1.25%/yr** (some 2%+); a US stock index fund
  ≈ **0.15%/yr** → roughly a **1.1%/yr** head start.
- Against ~9%/yr stock returns, giving up ~1.1% ≈ throwing away **~12% of expected
  return** every year (worse after tax).
- An index fund can't underperform its own index; an active manager can (wrong
  bets, cash at the wrong time, high fees).
- Indexing is *most* compelling for **bonds and large conservative US stocks**,
  where beating the market is hardest.

How much to index is personal taste: all-index (lock in market returns), none (if
you enjoy manager-picking and accept the odds), or a happy medium.

## Step 6 — Execution

**How many fund families:** one family = less paperwork but you sacrifice a little
because no family is best at everything; a discount brokerage gives many families
on one statement (small transaction fees on the better funds). The book's own
approach: a big fund company as a hub + discount brokerage for smaller families.

**Number of funds:** funds are already diversified, so you don't need many. A
hybrid fund / fund of funds can be a whole allocation by itself. As balances grow
(~$300k split three ways), use **2–3 funds per category** so one bad pick can't
sink a sleeve. Never own more funds than you have time to monitor.

**Minimum per fund:** aim for **$3,000–$5,000** when you can. A typical ~$10/yr
account maintenance fee is **2%/yr on a $500 balance** — some firms waive it above
a threshold (~$5,000).

**Prefer "purebred" funds** (~100% one asset type) so allocation math is a simple
multiply. Note even self-labeled purebreds may hold up to ~20% off-type. To fit a
hybrid to a target bond %, solve `hybrid_bond_fraction × X = target_bond%` for X.
(e.g. hit 25% bonds with a 40%-bond hybrid → 0.4X = 0.25 → X ≈ 60% in the hybrid.)

**Small balances:** buy a hybrid/fund-of-funds close to your target now, refine
later; or add one fund at a time, each year first topping up the asset type
currently doing *worst* (buying on sale) — picking a good fund within it.

**Lump sum vs. dollar-cost averaging (DCA):** for a windfall, DCA eases money in
on a schedule while the rest keeps earning interest.
- Pros: eases into risk, buys cheaper if prices fall, avoids "why didn't I wait?"
  regret. DCA through the worst decade (1928–38) still averaged **7%/yr**.
- Cons: if markets rise you'll wish you'd gone faster; you may lose nerve on the
  way down; complicates cost-basis tracking outside retirement accounts.
- Run it well: **automate it**, ideally **quarterly, early in the quarter**
  (Jan 1 / Apr 1 / Jul 1 / Oct 1). Big cautious investors may spread over **2–5
  years** (parking the rest in CDs/T-bills). Parking a windfall in a money market
  fund while you decide is fine — better than rushing into something you don't
  understand.

**Existing holdings:** keep good non-fund investments (count them in your
allocation), dump high-fee laggards, and mind that appreciated non-retirement
assets held **> 12 months** get long-term capital-gains rates.

## Worked end-to-end example

35-year-old, middle-of-the-road, investing $10,000 in a taxable account for
retirement:
1. **Allocation:** bonds = 35 − 10 = **25%**; stocks = 110 − 35 = **75%**.
2. **Home/abroad:** intl = 33% × 75% ≈ **25%** of portfolio → US stocks = **50%**.
   Final mix: **25% bonds / 50% US stocks / 25% international**.
3. **Dollars (purebreds):** $2,500 bonds, $5,000 US stock, $2,500 international.
4. **Strategy:** low-cost index funds for the bond and large-US sleeves; buy-and-hold.
5. **Tax note (taxable account):** see `tax-and-maintenance.md` — consider
   tax-friendly/tax-managed or muni options if in a high bracket.
