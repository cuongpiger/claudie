# Fund Screening & Evaluation

Source: *Mutual Funds For Dummies* Ch. 7. KB depth: `[[Ch07 - Finding the Best Funds]]`.

Use this to judge whether a **specific** fund or ETF is worth buying, to interpret
an expense ratio or load, to compare two funds, or to react sanely to a hyped
"hot" fund. Run every candidate through three filters, **in this order** — cost is
first because it's the only thing you can know for certain in advance.

## The 3-filter checklist

### Filter 1 — Costs (the decisive, knowable factor)
You can never know a fund's future return, but you can always know its costs, and
costs are subtracted from returns every year regardless of performance. So screen
on cost first.

**a) Loads (sales commissions) — avoid entirely.**
- A load is a commission (typically **4%–8.5%**) paid to a broker out of *your*
  invested dollars. It buys nothing in fund quality; no study shows loads buy
  better managers. Load funds also tend to carry **higher** ongoing expenses, not
  lower.
- **Buy no-load funds only.** The only reliable way to confirm truly no-load is
  the **prospectus** (marketing materials obscure it).

**b) Hidden loads — the A/B/C/D share-class trap.** Same fund and manager, different
ways to charge you:
- **Class A** — front-end load (sample prospectus: 6.5% deducted upfront).
- **Class B** — back-end/deferred load that fades over ~5–7 years, *but* raises
  annual expenses (up to **+1%/yr** in 12b-1 marketing fees). Usually costs more
  overall.
- **Class C/D** — ongoing 12b-1 commissions; often pricier than an old upfront load.
- Avoid all of these; **avoid 12b-1 fees** generally.

**c) Operating expenses (the unavoidable cost) — minimize.**
- Quoted as an annual % of assets, listed in the prospectus as **"Total Fund
  Operating Expenses."** Deducted daily before you see any return.
- How much expenses matter by fund type:

| Fund type | Expense ratio matters… | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Money market | **Critically** | Near-identical, efficiently-priced holdings → cost largely *is* the return |
| Bond | **Very much** | Same logic; similar securities |
| Stock | **Meaningfully** | Stocks avg ~10%/yr; +1.5% expense ≈ giving up ~15% of expected annual return |

- **High expenses do not buy higher returns** — higher-cost funds tend to earn
  *less*, and a high-cost manager may take extra risk to overcome the drag, so you
  get lower returns *and* more risk. Watch for temporary expense "reimbursements"
  on new funds that later vanish.

**Quick cost benchmarks to reach for** (illustrative, from the book's era — verify
current market norms):
- Money-market / bond index or low-cost funds: well under **~0.5%**, ideally
  ~0.2%.
- US stock index fund: around **~0.15%**; the average active US stock fund ran
  **~1.25%**. Treat expense ratios approaching/above ~1% on a plain-vanilla fund as
  a red flag unless there's a strong specific reason.

### Filter 2 — Performance vs. risk (never look at return alone)
- **"Past performance is no guarantee of future results" is the point, not
  boilerplate.** High returns are often just high risk — those same funds fall
  hardest in downturns. Always ask what risk was taken to earn a return, and
  confirm you're comfortable with that risk.
- **Ignore one-year numbers** (dominated by luck); favor 5- and 10-year records.
- **The star-fund trap:** of the #1 top-performing stock/bond funds in each of the
  last 20 years, **~80% later trailed their peer-group average** over the next
  5–10 years. Media-hyped stars (CGM Focus, Van Wagoner Emerging Growth, Fidelity
  Growth Strategies) went on to crater. A recent spectacular run is a reason for
  skepticism, not enthusiasm.
- **Compare apples-to-apples** to the *correct* benchmark and peer group — not the
  flattering one the fund's marketers picked. A 15% return is poor if the right
  index did 25%; don't measure an international fund against a US index. Be wary of
  a "#1 short-term bond fund" that got there by holding riskier
  BBB/intermediate-like bonds.

### Filter 3 — Manager & company expertise
- The manager matters, but the **gap between a "star" and an average manager is
  small** — maybe ~2%/yr (e.g. 15% vs. 13% in a decade when large US stocks
  averaged 13%). And if a star leaves, you're stuck with the fund.
- So **weigh the fund company's depth and track record** as much as the individual.
  Different firms are strong in different categories.
- **Avoid** inexperienced fund companies and gimmicky "newfangled" funds from
  marketing departments — they usually carry extra risk and high expenses.

## How to deliver a verdict

When the user hands you a fund, respond with a short structured judgment:
1. **Costs** — load? (kill it if yes); expense ratio vs. the type-appropriate
   benchmark above. If the fund names a **share class (A/B/C/D)**, say plainly what
   that class means — e.g. "Class A is the front-end-load share class" — so the
   user connects the cost to the label rather than having to infer it.
2. **Performance vs. risk** — is the record long enough, and judged against the
   right peer index? Any sign the returns came from outsized risk?
3. **Company/manager** — reputable firm with category depth?
4. **Verdict** — buy / pass / "need the prospectus to confirm true costs," with the
   single most important reason stated plainly.

If you're missing the expense ratio or load, say what to look up (the prospectus
line "Total Fund Operating Expenses") rather than guessing.

## Common myths to debunk on request
- "The fund company pays my commission, not you" → it comes from *your* money.
- "You get what you pay for — load funds have better managers" → no evidence; the
  load goes to the broker.
- "No-loads must have higher annual fees" → reverse is true on average.
- "I'll do a financial plan to find your needs" → never pay a *salesperson* for a
  plan; hire a fee-only (fiduciary) adviser if you want advice on no-loads.
