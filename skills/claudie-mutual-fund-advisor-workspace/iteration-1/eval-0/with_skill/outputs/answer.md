# Splitting your $85k rollover IRA

Nice move getting that old 401(k) into an IRA — you keep the tax shelter and usually get far more (and cheaper) fund choices. Here's how I'd think about the split, using the framework from Eric Tyson's *Mutual Funds For Dummies*.

## The inputs that drive the answer

- **Age:** 42
- **Time horizon:** ~25 years (you won't touch it until retirement) — this is squarely long-term money
- **Risk personality:** middle-of-the-road (your words)
- **Account type:** IRA (tax-sheltered), so tax placement doesn't complicate anything here — all interest, dividends, and gains grow tax-deferred, which actually makes bonds and rebalancing painless inside this account

Because the horizon is 25 years, you're firmly in "stocks + longer-term bonds" territory. Long-term money can ride out stock volatility because it has decades to recover — historically stocks have averaged roughly **9%/yr over the very long run** (with plenty of −20% years mixed in), and that average only accrues to people who stay invested through the rough patches.

## The allocation math (so you can redo it yourself)

For a long-term goal, the book starts from your age and adjusts by risk personality. For **middle-of-the-road**:

- **Bonds = age − 10 = 42 − 10 = 32%**
- **Stocks = 110 − age = 110 − 42 = 68%**

(Those two must sum to 100 — 32 + 68 = 100. ✓)

Next, split the stock sleeve between home and abroad. Concentrating all your stock money in one country — even the US — is riskier than spreading it globally. For middle-of-the-road, the guideline is **one-third of stocks overseas**:

- **International = 33% × 68% ≈ 22%** of the whole portfolio
- **US stocks = 68% − 22% ≈ 46%**

### Your target mix

| Sleeve | % of portfolio | Dollars (of $85k) |
|---|---|---|
| US stocks | ~46% | ~$39,000 |
| International stocks | ~22% | ~$19,000 |
| Bonds | ~32% | ~$27,000 |
| **Total** | **100%** | **$85,000** |

Round these however you like — being off by a percent or two changes nothing.

## Which funds to actually buy

**Lean on low-cost index funds, especially for the bond and large US stock sleeves.** Index funds beat roughly **75% of comparable active funds over 10+ years**, and the reason is almost entirely cost. Costs are the one lever you fully control — they're subtracted from your return whether the fund does well or badly. A typical active US stock fund runs ~1.25%/yr in expenses; a broad US stock index fund runs closer to ~0.15%. That ~1.1%/yr head start, against ~9% expected returns, is like throwing away over 10% of your return every year — and it compounds brutally over 25 years. An index fund also can't underperform its own index the way an active manager can with bad bets.

A clean, "purebred" three-fund lineup (each ~100% one asset type, so the math stays a simple multiply) covers you completely:

1. **Total US stock market index fund** — ~$39,000
2. **Total international stock index fund** — ~$19,000
3. **Total (intermediate-term) bond index fund** — ~$27,000

You do **not** need more funds than that — mutual funds are already diversified internally, and at $85k three funds is plenty to monitor. Each sleeve is comfortably above the ~$3,000–$5,000-per-fund minimum the book suggests, so you won't get nibbled by small-balance maintenance fees. As the balance grows well into six figures down the road, you *could* split a sleeve across 2–3 funds so one bad pick can't sink it — but that's a later refinement, not something to worry about now.

**One-fund shortcut:** if you'd rather not manage three pieces, a single **target-date retirement fund** (aimed at roughly the year you turn ~65) or a balanced "fund of funds" does the allocation *and* the automatic de-risking for you in one holding. The trade-offs: check its expense ratio (some are cheap index-based, some aren't), and its glide path may be more conservative than 68% stocks today. But for a hands-off investor it's a perfectly legitimate whole-portfolio in one ticker.

## How to deploy the money

Since this is a **rollover of money already invested**, you generally don't need to agonize over lump-sum vs. dollar-cost-averaging — it was already in the market, and stepping out to "wait for a better time" is just market timing in disguise. **Buy-and-hold beats market timing**, which only ever looks easy in hindsight. I'd put it to work in your target mix now (or over a few weeks if a big single-day move makes you uneasy) and then leave it alone.

## Ongoing

- **Rebalance about once a year** back to your targets. Inside an IRA this is free and has no tax consequences, which is a real advantage — you can trim what's run up and top up what's lagged without a tax bill.
- **Drift your stock share down slowly as you age.** The same formula naturally does this: at 50 it'd be ~40% bonds / 60% stocks, at 55 ~45%/55%, and so on. No need to touch it more than yearly.
- **Don't forget to keep contributing** if you're eligible — a rollover doesn't use up your annual IRA contribution room, and 25 more years of contributions matters as much as the allocation.

## A couple of caveats

- I'm giving educational guidance grounded in the book's framework, **not licensed financial advice.** I've assumed this $85k is genuinely long-term money you won't need before retirement, that your emergency reserve and any high-interest debt are already handled (get the financial foundation right *before* investing is rule #1), and that you and your spouse are comfortable with a stock-heavy mix that can drop 20%+ in a bad year without you bailing out. If a −20% year would make you sell, dial the stock share down a notch toward "play it safe" — the best allocation is the one you'll actually stick with.
- Contribution limits, tax rules, and specific fund expense ratios change over time — verify current figures before acting.
- For a $85k rollover plus long-term planning, a one-time check-in with a **fee-only (fiduciary) advisor** can be worth it, though the plan above is deliberately simple enough to run yourself.
