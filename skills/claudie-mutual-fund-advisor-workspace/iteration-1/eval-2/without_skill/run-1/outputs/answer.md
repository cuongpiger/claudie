# Getting Started: Debt, Safety Net, then Investing

First, the good news: at 28 with the instinct to invest, you're thinking about the right things at the right time. Time is the single biggest advantage you have. But the order in which you do things matters a lot, and the math points to a clear sequence.

*(This is educational information, not personalized financial or tax advice. Consider confirming specifics with a fee-only fiduciary advisor or a tax professional for your situation.)*

---

## The core tension: 19% debt vs. index fund returns

You want to invest, and that's a great instinct. But let's be honest about the numbers.

- Your credit card charges **19% APR**. Paying it off is a **guaranteed, tax-free 19% return** on every dollar you throw at it.
- A diversified stock index fund has historically returned roughly **7–10% per year on average** — but that's variable, not guaranteed, and can be negative for years at a time.

No index fund reliably beats a guaranteed 19%. So mathematically, **crushing the credit card debt is your highest-return "investment" right now.** It feels less exciting than buying an index fund, but it's the same thing as earning 19% risk-free.

The one nuance: don't go fully "all-in on debt with $0 in the bank," because the reason people fall into credit card debt in the first place is having no cushion when life throws a surprise. So we build a small buffer alongside.

---

## A step-by-step order of operations

### Step 1 — Build a starter emergency fund ($1,000–$2,000)
Before aggressively attacking the debt, park a small cushion in a **high-yield savings account (HYSA)** — many currently pay in the ballpark of 4% APY. This keeps a flat tire or a dental bill from going right back onto the 19% card. Think of it as insurance for your debt payoff plan.

### Step 2 — Capture any free 401(k) match (if you have one)
If your employer offers a 401(k) match (e.g., "we match 100% of the first 4%"), contribute **just enough to get the full match** — even while paying down debt. A 100% match is an instant 100% return, which beats even your 19% debt. If there's no match, skip this and go straight to Step 3.

### Step 3 — Attack the $6k credit card debt aggressively
Throw every spare dollar at the card. Two popular methods:
- **Avalanche** (cheapest): pay minimums on everything, then dump extra on the highest-APR balance. Best if your $6k is spread across cards.
- **Snowball** (most motivating): pay off the smallest balance first for a psychological win.

Since it sounds like this is one main 19% balance, avalanche essentially means "pay it off as fast as you can." Also consider a **0% APR balance-transfer card** if you qualify — it can pause the interest for 12–18 months (watch for the ~3–5% transfer fee) and let 100% of your payments hit principal.

At $6k and 19%, if you can put ~$500/month toward it, you'd be debt-free in roughly **13–14 months** and save a meaningful chunk of interest.

### Step 4 — Build a full emergency fund (3–6 months of expenses)
Once the card is gone, redirect those payments into your HYSA until you have **3–6 months of essential expenses** saved. This is the foundation that lets you invest without being forced to sell in a downturn.

### Step 5 — Now invest in index funds, tax-efficiently
With no high-interest debt and a real safety net, you can invest with confidence and a long time horizon.

## How to actually start investing in index funds

**Use tax-advantaged accounts first — this is the biggest lever for keeping taxes low.**

1. **Roth IRA** (2025 limit: $7,000/yr if under 50). You contribute after-tax dollars, and all growth and withdrawals in retirement are **completely tax-free**. Ideal for a 28-year-old whose income (and tax bracket) will likely rise. Open one at Fidelity, Vanguard, or Schwab in ~15 minutes.
2. **401(k)** beyond the match, especially if you want to lower this year's taxable income (traditional) or you have Roth 401(k) access.
3. **Taxable brokerage account** only after you've maxed the tax-advantaged space, or if you need access to the money before retirement.

**What to buy — keep it boring and broad:**
- A **total US stock market index fund** (e.g., VTI / VTSAX / FSKAX) or an **S&P 500 index fund** (VOO / FXAIX).
- Optionally add a **total international index fund** and, later, bonds.
- A single **target-date fund** (e.g., "Target 2060") is a legitimate one-fund solution — it's automatically diversified and rebalances for you. Perfect if you'd rather not think about it.

Look for **expense ratios under ~0.10%**. The big index funds are essentially free these days, and low fees compound in your favor over decades.

**Automate it.** Set up an automatic monthly transfer into the account. Consistent, boring, automatic investing beats trying to time the market.

---

## Keeping taxes low — the practical checklist

- **Prioritize tax-advantaged accounts** (Roth IRA, 401(k)/HSA) over taxable ones. This is 90% of tax efficiency for most people.
- **HSA**, if you have a high-deductible health plan, is the most tax-advantaged account that exists (deductible in, tax-free growth, tax-free for medical) — worth using.
- In taxable accounts, favor **broad index funds/ETFs**, which are naturally tax-efficient (low turnover, few capital gains distributions). ETFs are generally slightly more tax-efficient than mutual funds.
- **Buy and hold.** Selling investments held under a year triggers higher **short-term capital gains** (taxed as ordinary income). Holding 1+ year qualifies for lower **long-term** rates.
- Don't chase frequent trading — it creates taxable events and rarely beats buy-and-hold.
- Later on, techniques like **tax-loss harvesting** and holding tax-inefficient assets (bonds, REITs) inside retirement accounts can help, but don't over-engineer this early.

## Your simple game plan

1. Save a **$1–2k starter cushion** in a high-yield savings account.
2. Grab any **401(k) employer match** (free money).
3. **Pay off the $6k at 19%** as fast as possible — this *is* a 19% return.
4. Build a **3–6 month emergency fund**.
5. **Open a Roth IRA**, buy a low-cost total-market or S&P 500 index fund (or a target-date fund), and **automate monthly contributions**.

The index funds will still be there in 12–18 months — and you'll be buying them with money that's no longer bleeding 19% interest, backed by a safety net that lets you stay invested through the ups and downs. That patience is exactly what makes long-term investing work.
