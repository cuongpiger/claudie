# Where to begin (and it's not index funds yet)

First: love the instinct to invest at 28. Time is the single biggest advantage you have, and wanting to use it puts you ahead of most people. So I'm not going to talk you out of investing — I'm going to get you to the starting line in the right order, because the order is what makes the difference over a decade.

Here's the honest framing from the *Mutual Funds For Dummies* pre-investing checklist: **the most common beginner mistake is buying funds before the financial foundation is ready.** There are three "gates" to clear before a dollar goes into an index fund, and you're sitting right on top of the two that matter most: high-interest debt and an emergency reserve.

Let me walk your actual numbers.

---

## Step 1 — Kill the credit card debt first (this IS your best investment)

You have **$6,000 at 19% APR**. Paying that off isn't a detour away from investing — it's the highest-return, lowest-risk investment available to you right now. Here's the math that makes it non-negotiable:

- Stocks have returned roughly **~9%/year** on average over two centuries — and only to people who stay invested through the ugly years.
- Your card charges **19%/year, guaranteed, with no volatility.**
- Paying off the balance is a **guaranteed, tax-free 19% return.** No index fund can promise you that. You cannot reliably out-earn 19% interest with an ~9% expected return — you'd be borrowing at 19% to chase 9%, which is a guaranteed loss of ~10%/year on that money.

So every spare dollar goes at the card until it's gone. On $6,000 at 19%, if you can throw ~$500/month at it you're done in about 13–14 months; ~$650/month gets you there in about 10. Whatever the exact number, treat this as your "investment plan" for roughly the next year.

One nuance from the book: it's fine to keep a *small* cash cushion while you attack the debt (see Step 2) so a flat tire doesn't send you straight back to the card. But don't let a big pile of "someday" cash sit at 0–4% while 19% compounds against you.

## Step 2 — Build a starter emergency reserve (in parallel / right after)

You said no emergency fund. That's the other gate. Without it, the next surprise — car repair, medical bill, gap between jobs — goes back onto the 19% card and undoes your progress.

- **Target: 3–6 months of living expenses**, held in a **money market fund** (or equivalent high-yield savings) — something stable and liquid, *not* stocks. This is short-term money, so it stays safe by design; the whole point is that it's there and unmoved when you need it.
- Because you're mid-payoff, a sensible sequence is: build a small **$1,000–$1,500 starter cushion** first so you're not one emergency away from the card, then **blitz the $6k debt**, then **finish filling the reserve to a full 3–6 months** before you start investing.
- If your income is erratic (freelance, commission, no family safety net), aim toward the **higher end — up to a full year**. If it's a steady salary, 3–4 months is reasonable.

## Step 3 — Two quick foundation checks while you're at it

- **Insurance:** one uninsured catastrophe can wipe out years of investing. Make sure you have health coverage, and — if anyone depends on your income — long-term **disability** insurance especially (it's the coverage people your age most often skip and most need).
- **A 401(k) match, if your employer offers one:** this is the one exception to "debt first." An employer match is an instant 50–100% return — free money you shouldn't leave on the table. If you have a match, contribute *just enough to capture the full match* even while paying down the card, then send everything else at the debt. If there's no match, debt payoff wins outright.

---

## Then — and this is where the fun starts — the index-fund plan

Once the card is at $0 and your reserve is funded, you'll actually be in a *stronger* position to invest than most people who skip these steps, because your cash flow is now free and you won't panic-sell the first time the market drops. Here's the shape of it, so you know what you're working toward:

**Use tax-advantaged accounts first — this is your #1 tax lever.** For long-term money like retirement, the biggest tax savings by far come from *where* you invest, not from clever fund picking:

- **401(k)/403(b)** (especially with a match) and/or a **Roth IRA.** In these, growth compounds without you owing tax on dividends or gains along the way. A Roth in particular is a strong fit at 28 — you pay tax on the contribution now while your income/bracket is likely lower, and qualified withdrawals in retirement are tax-free.
- Max out the tax-advantaged space you have access to *before* investing in a regular taxable brokerage account. This single habit does more for your after-tax returns than any fund-level tax trick.

**Your allocation** (a starting point — retirement is a 7+ year, really 30+ year, horizon so it's stock-heavy). Using the book's age-based formula for a "middle-of-the-road" risk personality:

- Bonds = age − 10 = 28 − 10 = **~18%**
- Stocks = 110 − age = 110 − 28 = **~82%**
- Of the stock sleeve, put about a third overseas: 33% × 82% ≈ **~27% international**, leaving **~55% US stocks**.
- So a rough target: **~55% US stock index / ~27% international stock index / ~18% bond index.**

If a 20–30% drop in a given year would genuinely make you want to bail, start one notch more conservative ("play it safe": bonds = age = ~28%) and migrate toward more stocks as your comfort grows. Gradual beats abrupt. If you're comfortable with volatility and want maximum long-run growth, you can lean more aggressive. That dial is genuinely your call.

**Why index funds are the right instinct:** low-cost index funds beat about **75% of comparable active funds over 10+ years**, mostly on cost. A typical active US stock fund runs ~1.25%/year in expenses; a broad US stock index fund runs closer to ~0.15%. That ~1.1%/year gap, against ~9% expected returns, is like throwing away ~12% of your return every year — and it compounds. Favor **no-load, low-expense-ratio index funds**, and **buy-and-hold** rather than trying to time the market.

**Keeping taxes low, in order of impact:**
1. **Tax-advantaged accounts first** (401(k), Roth IRA) — the big one, covered above.
2. **In any taxable account, index funds/ETFs are naturally tax-efficient** — they trade little, so they throw off few taxable capital-gains distributions. This is a real edge over active funds beyond just fees.
3. **Hold for the long term.** Gains on investments held **over a year** get preferential long-term capital-gains rates; under a year they're taxed as ordinary income. Buy-and-hold is tax-smart automatically.
4. **Municipal (tax-free) bond funds** only make sense in a *taxable* account and only if you're in a high federal bracket (roughly **28%+**) — at 28 with modest income and no debt-free surplus yet, that's probably not you, so don't reach for them. And never hold muni/Treasury funds *inside* a retirement account — it wastes the shelter.
5. Small timing point for later: **don't buy a fund right before its year-end distribution** (usually December) in a taxable account — you'd owe tax on a payout that just lowers the share price, gaining nothing.

---

## Your actual roadmap, in one glance

1. **Now:** stash a small **$1,000–$1,500** starter cushion in a money market / high-yield savings account. Capture any **employer 401(k) match**.
2. **Next ~10–14 months:** throw every spare dollar at the **$6,000 / 19% card** until it's gone. This is your best "investment" this year.
3. **After that:** finish building the **3–6 month emergency reserve** (up to a year if your income is lumpy). Confirm health + disability insurance.
4. **Then:** open/fund a **Roth IRA and/or 401(k)**, and start buying **low-cost, no-load index funds** at roughly **55% US stock / 27% international / 18% bond**, buy-and-hold. Automate the contributions.

You're not behind by doing it in this order — you're doing it *right*. Clearing 19% debt and building a reserve first is exactly what lets you stay invested calmly through the market drops that shake everyone else out.
